Project: Moving Apps from VMs to Kubernetes

A realistic scenario: a company runs containerised microservices with Docker Compose on virtual machines, and you must move them to Kubernetes. This lesson is the plan: six levels, from a spreadsheet to a multi-region production setup.

This project reuses ideas from other lessons: Deployments, Ingress, RBAC, StatefulSets, monitoring. Here we focus on how to sequence the migration.

The scenario

You join a large company as a DevOps engineer. Their application is made of many microservices (think of an online shop: UI, payments, transactions, shipping, recommendations). Each service is already in a Docker image. Today they run with Docker Compose on VMs, with a load balancer in front. Nobody on the team knows Kubernetes, and you must "move us to Kubernetes".

Watch outThe classic mistake: create a cluster, pick a random service, and deploy it. A migration takes weeks or months, so work in levels, each with a clear goal.

What changes from Compose to Kubernetes

In Docker ComposeIn Kubernetes
a service with image:a Deployment (or StatefulSet for databases)

Level 0: requirement gathering

No cluster yet. You collect facts in a spreadsheet and share it with developers and management.

  1. List the microservices. What looks like one app to a user is many services (UI, payments, its database, its config service...).
  2. List the teams. Services are owned by teams (payments, shipping, UI). This later becomes your namespace layout.
  3. Rank by business impact: less critical, medium, important, critical. You migrate the least critical first, so mistakes are cheap.
  4. Measure current resource use. The most important step. Read CPU, memory and disk per service from Prometheus/Grafana, or from CloudWatch or your cloud billing dashboard. This sizes the cluster. Otherwise you may build a 50-CPU cluster you don't need.
  5. Estimate cost. Compare today's VM bill with what EKS, AKS or GKE would cost, using the provider's price calculator. Management cares about the balance of cost and performance.

Sheet columns that work well: service, team, CPU, memory, business impact, onboarding priority, estimated cost, environment readiness (not ready / in progress / ready), timeline.

TipDiscuss the sheet with developers and managers before you start. A plan only you have seen is a plan nobody will support.

Level 1: proof of concept (about 2 to 4 weeks)

You don't yet know whether each service even runs well on Kubernetes. Prove it on a small slice.

  • Pick 15 to 20 services, a few from every impact group. Include both stateless apps and stateful ones (a database, a cache, a queue).
  • Create a small cluster (for example 3 control-plane and 3 worker nodes of 8 CPU / 8 GB). Managed services run the control plane for you.
  • Write manifests: Deployments, or StatefulSets and DaemonSets where needed, plus Services and an Ingress. For the ingress controller, the easiest start is your cloud's own (ALB on AWS).
  • Test traffic and ask the QE team to run their tests.
  • Fix what breaks. Pods in CrashLoopBackOff usually need correct config, or liveness and readiness probes.

If you want to practise Level 1 on your own machine, a local cluster from kind works for the manifests part.

Level 2: the dev cluster

Now you build for real, using the numbers from level 0.

Sizing

  • Control plane: three nodes minimum (managed services handle this).
  • Workers: at least three, with a little more CPU and memory than the total you measured. Example: you need 40 CPU and 60 GB, so use three nodes of 16 CPU / 24 GB (48 CPU / 72 GB).
  • A cluster can scale to thousands of nodes (the official guideline is 5000 nodes, 110 Pods per node), but more nodes means more work during upgrades, which go node by node (see Lesson 16). Don't create 10 nodes when 3 do.

Enter the totals from your sheet and a node count. The helper adds headroom and suggests a node size.

Namespaces, quotas and limits

Create one namespace per team. That keeps teams apart and lets you grant each developer access only to their own namespace using RBAC. A UI developer has no reason to touch the payments database.

Then stop one team (or one leaky Pod) from eating the whole cluster:

  • ResourceQuota caps what a namespace may use in total.
  • requests and limits (and optionally a LimitRange for defaults) cap each Pod.
apiVersion: v1
kind: ResourceQuota
metadata:
  name: payments-quota
  namespace: payments
spec:
  hard:
    requests.cpu: "8"
    requests.memory: 16Gi
    limits.cpu: "12"
    limits.memory: 24Gi
    pods: "60"

Set each namespace's quota a little above what that team measured. Let developers and testers use the dev cluster for about a month before moving on.

Level 3: staging (or QE)

Dev is unstable by design because developers change it daily. When production has a problem you need a stable place to reproduce it. That is staging. There are two ways to build it:

Same cluster, separate namespaces

Cheaper. Namespaces like dev-payments and stage-payments. But RBAC must be strict, or developers will disturb staging too.

A separate cluster

Costs more but gives clean isolation. A replica of dev with more resources, closer to production. This is the better choice if the budget allows.

How many environments you need depends on risk. A bank may have dev, QE, staging, UAT and pre-prod. A small product company may need only pre-prod and prod.

Level 4: production

Setup is similar, with two big additions.

  • Multiple Availability Zones. An AZ is a data centre. Spread nodes over three of them (for example 1a, 1b, 1c) so losing one doesn't take you down.
  • Pods must spread too. Nodes in three zones don't help if all replicas land in one. Use topology spread constraints:
spec:
  topologySpreadConstraints:
  - maxSkew: 1
    topologyKey: topology.kubernetes.io/zone
    whenUnsatisfiable: DoNotSchedule
    labelSelector:
      matchLabels:
        app: payments

(This goes in the Pod template of your Deployment. For other placement tools see Lesson 21.) In production you can be more generous with sizing or use autoscaling (Cluster Autoscaler or Karpenter). Also set up observability (Prometheus and Grafana), probes, and tested requests and limits before you take real traffic.

Level 5: scale production

If customers are worldwide, a US cluster is reachable from Asia but with higher latency. For high availability and speed, run several clusters, one per region (US, Asia, Europe). Each has its own Ingress. In front, put a global load balancer or DNS routing (such as Route 53 latency or geo routing), which sends each user to the nearest cluster.

It doesn't end here. Next come Helm charts (Lesson 27), security (Lesson 23), GitOps, service mesh and policy tools, and so on.

Interview angle"How would you migrate 100 services from VMs to Kubernetes?" Answer in levels: gather requirements and measure usage, run a proof of concept on a mixed sample, build dev, then staging, then multi-AZ production, then multi-region. Mention namespace per team, quotas, probes, and migrating least-critical services first.

Check yourself

Which service should you migrate first?
Rank services by business impact and start with the least critical, working up to critical ones.
What is the difference between a ResourceQuota and Pod limits?
Quotas keep a team within its share of the cluster; requests and limits keep each Pod within its share of that.
You have nodes in three zones, but all replicas of one Deployment are on one node. What helps?
Topology spread constraints tell the scheduler to place replicas across zones.

Key takeaways

  • Migrate in levels: requirements, proof of concept, dev, staging, production, scale.
  • Measure real CPU, memory and cost from your monitoring before sizing anything.
  • Prove a mixed sample of 15 to 20 services before building environments.
  • Namespace per team, with RBAC, ResourceQuota and Pod limits for fair sharing.
  • Production needs multiple zones (and spread constraints); multi-region means several clusters behind DNS or a global load balancer.
  • Share your plan with developers and management.
