Deployment Strategies

How do you ship version 2 of an app without users noticing? You will learn why simply deleting and reinstalling causes downtime, and how rolling update, canary and blue/green releases each reduce the risk, with a step-through demo to compare them.

Deployments, ReplicaSets and rolling updates are introduced in Lesson 5. Ingress is covered in Lesson 9. Here we compare the release strategies.

Why do you need a strategy?

Imagine a Payments service running version 1 on 4 replicas. The easy way to release version 2 is to delete v1, then install v2. If deleting takes 2 minutes and the new Pods need 5 more minutes to be ready, payments are down for several minutes. That means lost revenue and unhappy users.

A deployment strategy is a plan for replacing the old version with the new one so downtime is zero or close to it, and so a bad release does little harm.

RecreateStop all v1, then start v2Yes (strategy.type: Recreate)
Rolling updateReplace Pods graduallyYes (the default)
CanarySend a small share of real traffic to v2No: needs an ingress controller or service mesh
Blue/greenRun v2 beside v1, then flip all trafficNo: switch a Service or Ingress

1. Rolling update (the default)

If you use a Deployment, you are already using this. Change the image and Kubernetes adds one new v2 Pod, waits until it is ready, removes one v1 Pod, and repeats until all Pods are v2.

Two settings control the pace. Both default to 25%:

  • maxSurge: how many extra Pods may exist above the desired count (25% of 4 = 1).
  • maxUnavailable: how many Pods may be missing at once (25% of 4 = 1).
apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata:
  name: payments
spec:
  replicas: 4
  strategy:
    type: RollingUpdate
    rollingUpdate:
      maxSurge: 25%
      maxUnavailable: 25%
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: payments
  template:
    metadata:
      labels:
        app: payments
    spec:
      containers:
        - name: payments
          image: myrepo/payments:v1
          readinessProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /health
              port: 8080

Trigger a release and watch it:

kubectl set image deployment/payments payments=myrepo/payments:v2
kubectl get pods -w
kubectl rollout status deployment/payments
kubectl rollout undo deployment/payments     # go back if needed
Always add a readiness probeWithout a readiness probe, Kubernetes treats a Pod as ready as soon as its container starts. It may then remove an old Pod even though the new one cannot serve traffic yet.

What if the new version is broken? Say you set a non-existent image. The new Pod fails (see ImagePullBackOff) but the other three v1 Pods keep serving, and the Service only sends traffic to healthy Pods. Users are not hurt.

Limits: it is "almost" zero downtime, not guaranteed, because a new version may need time to connect to databases or other services. You also have no control over speed or who sees v2: within minutes everyone does.

2. Canary

Why do other strategies exist if rolling update already avoids downtime? Because of a testing gap. v2 is tested in dev, QA and pre-production, but those have less data and smaller clusters than production. Some bugs only show up in production.

Think of it like…Miners once carried a canary into the mine: if the bird fell ill, they knew to retreat before people were hurt. A canary release sends a few real users to the new version first.

You run v1 and v2 together, and tell the load balancer to send, say, 10% of traffic to v2 and 90% to v1. If v2 misbehaves, only a few users are affected and you set the weight back to 0. If it looks good, raise it to 30%, 50%, then 100%. You can hold at 10% for days while the QE team runs tests.

With the NGINX ingress controller, you create two of everything (Deployment, Service, Ingress), and mark the second Ingress as a canary with annotations:

apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: Ingress
metadata:
  name: echo-canary
  annotations:
    nginx.ingress.kubernetes.io/canary: "true"
    nginx.ingress.kubernetes.io/canary-weight: "10"
spec:
  ingressClassName: nginx
  rules:
    - host: echo.prod.mydomain.com
      http:
        paths:
          - path: /
            pathType: Prefix
            backend:
              service:
                name: echo-canary      # the v2 Service
                port:
                  number: 80

The main Ingress (no canary annotation) points to the v1 Service with the same host. Test by sending 10 requests: about 9 hit v1 and 1 hits v2. Then change the weight with kubectl edit ingress echo-canary (the controller reconfigures itself in seconds). The v2 side needs fewer replicas, since it gets less traffic.

Cost: more YAML and moving parts. Use it when even a small outage or bad experience is costly.

3. Blue/green

Keep the current version (blue) untouched and deploy a complete, identical copy of the new version (green) next to it, with its own Service. Test green privately. Then, in one step, change the Ingress (or Service selector) from the blue Service to the green Service. All traffic moves at once. If something is wrong, point it back to blue: an instant rollback.

# in the Ingress backend, change:
service:
  name: payments-blue      # before
# to:
service:
  name: payments-green     # after: all traffic flips

Safest but most expensive: you pay for double the Pods (CPU and memory) for as long as both exist. For 100 services that is a lot, so few companies use it everywhere. Banks and financial services, where a quick rollback matters more than cost, often do.

Interview angle"What deployment strategies do you know?" Rolling update is the default; canary tests v2 on a slice of real production traffic with easy rollback; blue/green is the safest but doubles resource cost. Mention maxSurge/maxUnavailable and readiness probes, and that canary and blue/green need an ingress controller (or similar) because Kubernetes does not do them natively.

Check yourself

What are the default maxSurge and maxUnavailable of a rolling update?
Both default to 25%. With 4 replicas, that means one extra Pod and at most one unavailable Pod at a time.
Which strategy lets you test v2 on production with only about 10% of users?
Canary routes a weighted share of traffic to the new version, so a bug affects only a few users.
Why is blue/green used by fewer companies than canary?
It is very safe with instant rollback, but running two complete environments is expensive.

Key takeaways

  • Deleting and reinstalling causes downtime; a strategy replaces versions safely.
  • Rolling update is Kubernetes' default: gradual replacement controlled by maxSurge and maxUnavailable; add readiness probes.
  • Canary sends a small, adjustable share of real traffic to v2, so production testing is low-risk.
  • Blue/green runs two full copies and flips traffic in one step: safest, but costly.
  • Canary and blue/green are done with an ingress controller (annotations or a changed backend), not by Kubernetes alone.
