Services: Discovery & Load Balancing

Pods come and go, and every new Pod gets a new IP address. A Service gives your application one stable address, finds the right Pods by label, and spreads traffic across them. You'll see why it exists and how it works.

This lesson builds on Lesson 5: Deployments & ReplicaSets (self-healing and replicas) and Lesson 4: Pods (labels on Pods).

The problem: Pod IPs keep changing

A Deployment keeps, say, three replicas of your app running. Each Pod gets its own IP address from the cluster network, for example 172.16.3.4, 172.16.3.5 and 172.16.3.6.

Now imagine you give those three IPs to three other teams that need to call your app. Then one Pod crashes. The ReplicaSet does its job and starts a replacement, but the replacement is a new Pod with a new IP, maybe 172.16.3.8.

  • From your side everything looks healthy: three Pods are running.
  • From the other team's side the app is "down", because they are still calling 172.16.3.4.

Nobody is wrong. Pod IPs are dynamic: they are handed out when a Pod is created and are gone when it dies. Self-healing alone is not enough; you need something stable in front of the Pods.

The real world works the same way. When you open google.com, Google does not tell you "use server 100.64.2.7". You get one name, and a load balancer behind it decides which server answers. Kubernetes gives you that with a Service (short name: svc).

What a Service does for you

A Service is a stable front door for a group of Pods. It gives you three things:

1. Load balancing

Requests to the Service are spread across all healthy Pods behind it, so no single replica gets overloaded.

2. Service discovery

The Service finds its Pods by label, not by IP. New or replaced Pods are picked up automatically.

3. Exposure

Depending on its type, a Service can be reached only inside the cluster, from the node network, or from the internet.

Bonus: a stable name

Every Service gets a fixed IP and a DNS name, so other apps can call payments instead of an IP.

Service discovery with labels and selectors

You might ask: if the Service forwards to Pod IPs, doesn't it suffer from the same changing-IP problem? It would, if it tracked IPs by hand. Instead it uses labels and a selector.

  • A label is a key/value tag on an object, such as app: payments.
  • A selector is a question: "give me every Pod that has this label".

When the Deployment creates Pods, its Pod template stamps the same label on every Pod. When a Pod dies, the ReplicaSet creates a replacement from the same template, so the replacement carries the same label even though its IP is new. The Service only cares about the label, so it finds the new Pod on its own. Scale from 2 to 20 Pods and the Service follows, with no changes to the Service.

# Deployment: stamps the label on every Pod it creates
spec:
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: payments
  template:
    metadata:
      labels:
        app: payments        # <- the Pod label
---
# Service: selects Pods that carry that label
apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: payments
spec:
  selector:
    app: payments            # <- must match the Pod label
  ports:
    - port: 80
      targetPort: 8000
Watch outThe selector must match the labels in the Pod template (spec.template.metadata.labels), not just the Deployment's own labels. A one-character typo means the Service selects nothing and traffic goes nowhere, with no error message.

Under the hood: endpoints and kube-proxy

Two pieces make the magic work:

  1. Endpoints. Kubernetes continuously watches for Pods that match the selector (and are Ready) and keeps a list of their IP:port pairs. This list is stored as Endpoints / EndpointSlice objects. You can see it with kubectl get endpoints payments. A Pod that dies drops off the list; a new Pod is added.
  2. kube-proxy. This component runs on every node (see Lesson 2: Architecture). It watches Services and Endpoints and writes network rules on the node (by default iptables rules) that say "traffic to the Service IP should go to one of these Pod IPs".

The Service IP, called the ClusterIP, is virtual. No network card owns it; the node's rules rewrite the destination to a real Pod. The balancing is simple (roughly round-robin or random), not "smart". Features like sticky sessions or path routing need something more, which is why Ingress exists.

Inside the cluster, Services also get a DNS name, using the pattern <service>.<namespace>.svc.cluster.local. From a Pod in the same namespace you can just call http://payments; from another namespace use payments.default or the full name.

Service types at a glance

Every Service has a ClusterIP. The type field decides who else can reach it. Each type builds on the previous one. The next lesson, Services Deep Dive, runs all of them hands-on.

TypeWho can reach itTypical use
ClusterIP (default)Only things inside the clusterInternal calls, e.g. checkout to payments
NodePortAnyone who can reach the node IPs, on a port in 30000-32767Internal company users, quick tests
LoadBalancerAnyone on the internet, via a cloud load balancerPublic apps on a cloud provider
Think of it like…A company's front desk. Callers dial one number (the Service) and the receptionist connects them to whichever employee (Pod) is free. Employees can quit and be replaced, and the number never changes. ClusterIP is an internal extension, NodePort is a number for people inside the building, and LoadBalancer is a public phone number.
Interview angle"Why do we need Services?" Pod IPs are dynamic, so you need stable access (discovery), traffic spread across replicas (load balancing), and a way to expose the app (ClusterIP / NodePort / LoadBalancer). Mention that discovery uses labels and selectors, and kube-proxy programs the rules.

Check yourself

How does a Service keep track of which Pods to send traffic to?
Pods are matched by label via the selector. Kubernetes keeps the endpoint list up to date as Pods are created and removed.
You create a Service but kubectl get endpoints shows no addresses. What is the most likely cause?
An empty endpoint list means no Ready Pod matched the selector. Check for typos between the Service selector and the Pod template labels.
Which Service type is the default, and who can reach it?
If you do not set type, you get ClusterIP, which is reachable only from inside the cluster.

Key takeaways

  • Pod IPs change whenever a Pod is replaced, so never hand them out; put a Service in front.
  • A Service gives you load balancing, service discovery and a way to expose the app, plus a stable IP and DNS name.
  • Discovery works through labels and selectors, so replacements and new replicas are found automatically.
  • Endpoints list the matching Pod IPs, and kube-proxy turns that into network rules on each node.
  • Types: ClusterIP (inside), NodePort (node network), LoadBalancer (internet via cloud).
